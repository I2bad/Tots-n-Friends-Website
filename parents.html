Play and learning in the first five years

A lot happens before your child turns five. Here's what the research says, in plain words.

Things parents often tell us

Most of these sound sensible. The research says otherwise. Tap a card to see the facts.

  • Myth🎨

    “Play is just play. It's not real learning.”

    The truth

    Children in play-based programmes do better later in life, including at school, than children in programmes focused on academics. Play is how young children build language, number sense and problem-solving.

    Source: Trying Together; Lillio / Rae Pica

  • Myth📚

    “Academic skills should be the main focus of early education.”

    The truth

    Good programmes give feelings, friendships and physical skills the same weight as academics. Self-control, empathy and taking turns are what help most in the long run.

    Source: Trying Together

  • Myth⏰

    “The earlier you start formal academics, the better.”

    The truth

    Pushing formal lessons too early can backfire. Many academic programmes for young children don't suit how they develop. A child's own curiosity and readiness should set the pace, not a checklist.

    Source: Lillio / Rae Pica, award-winning ECE educator

  • Myth🧸

    “You need expensive educational toys for brain development.”

    The truth

    There is no scientific evidence that costly “educational” toys improve brain development, and they can overstimulate children. The best brain-builders are talking, reading and singing with your child. They cost nothing at all.

    Source: First 5 California

  • Myth🏠

    “Real learning only begins when my child starts school.”

    The truth

    You are your child's first and most important teacher. Reading together, answering their babbling, even peekaboo all count. Encouraging replies at story time can move a two-year-old's language ahead by up to 9 months.

    Source: First 5 California

What the studies found

Researchers have followed children for decades, in studies like the Perry Preschool Project and the Abecedarian Project. Good early education keeps paying off long after preschool ends.
